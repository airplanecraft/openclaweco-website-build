FAA Activates First Digital Air Traffic Control Towers in Florida and Alaska

November 8, 2026|3 Min Read
#faa#digital-tower#air-traffic-control#human-in-the-loop#ai-safety

The Federal Aviation Administration (#FAA) has announced that the first digital air traffic control towers in the US are officially operational, bringing new technology first to smaller airports in Florida and Alaska as the Trump administration presses ahead with updating the country's ageing aviation infrastructure.

The systems are now online at the Bartow executive airport (KBOW) in Florida and the Kenai municipal airport (PAEN) in Alaska, with five additional regional airports slated to receive the technology next.

The digital towers use advanced camera systems and digital displays to give air traffic controllers a continuous, 360-degree view of airport operations, including runways, taxiways and surrounding airspace, according to the agency. The FAA maintains that the technology is not intended to replace human controllers. Instead, the systems are designed to provide greater visibility and additional information to help controllers perform their existing duties.

"Our air traffic controllers continue to do the critical job of keeping aircraft safely separated, and we're giving them better technology to do that," said the FAA's administrator, Bryan Bedford, in a statement. "Digital towers provide controllers with greater visibility of airport activity and more information to make critical decisions," he added.

Bedford is scheduled to appear before the House transportation and infrastructure committee next week, where he will face questions about the progress and challenges surrounding the administration's ongoing $12.5bn modernization initiative. The effort aims to replace decades-old equipment still used throughout the aviation system, including copper telecommunications lines, floppy disks and paper strips used to track aircraft.

Congress approved $12.5bn over five years in June 2025 to support air traffic control reforms, but the Trump administration is seeking another $30bn from the legislative branch to fund additional improvements.

Concerns about the types of technology being implemented within air traffic control have been gaining traction alongside the increasing unease about artificial intelligence as a whole. In September, the FAA rolled out a new AI software tool called the Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories (Smart).

Trump has been heavily criticized for his response to a national shortage of air traffic controllers following last year's government shutdown. Staffing levels in control towers and many related aspects of US air traffic safety systems have been under especially close scrutiny since a midair collision between a passenger jet and a military helicopter on the outskirts of Washington DC in 2025 that killed 67 people. It was the deadliest crash in the US for more than 20 years and the authorities later deemed it "100% preventable".

Unlike general-purpose multi-agent frameworks such as CrewAI and AutoGen that pursue end-to-end automation, digital towers choose an "augment, not replace" path: camera arrays and digital displays serve as the perception layer, while controllers remain the core of the decision loop. Compared with the popular "#human-in-the-loop" pattern in the LangChain ecosystem, the FAA's approach is closer to "human-over-the-loop"—humans retain final veto power, but Agents handle continuous monitoring and anomaly alerts. The real challenge is not technical but regulatory: how much of the FAA's $12.5bn modernization plan can be translated into reusable Agent infrastructure standards will determine the industry-wide spillover value of this experiment.
